# OSHA 1910 / 1926 — Code-Reference Overview

## Learning Objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the learner will be able to:

- Identify the OSHA regulatory trigger that determines whether 29 CFR 1910 (general industry) or 29 CFR 1926 (construction) governs a given OSP work activity
- Locate the correct CFR part and section for confined space entry (manhole/handhole) and for electrical hazard approach distances at aerial work sites
- Explain why L4.13 provides code pointers only — field procedure execution is Topic 9

## Reading Content

### OSHA's Two Regulatory Universes for OSP Work

OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) regulates worker safety through two parallel regulatory frameworks under Title 29 of the Code of Federal Regulations:

- **29 CFR Part 1910** — Occupational Safety and Health Standards for **general industry** (maintenance, operation, inspection of existing facilities)
- **29 CFR Part 1926** — Safety and Health Regulations for **construction** (new construction, installation, modification of facilities)

For OSP fiber work, the applicability trigger is the nature of the work, not the location:

| Work type | OSHA part | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Installing new conduit, placing new cable, building new aerial strand | 1926 (construction) | New fiber route from hub to terminal |
| Performing splicing maintenance in an existing splice vault | 1910 (general industry) | Re-splicing an existing fiber plant |
| Inspecting existing cable plant, testing without modifying | 1910 (general industry) | OTDR testing of in-service cable |
| Performing make-ready on existing poles for new attachment | 1926 or 1910 depending on scope | Shifting existing attachments (existing-plant maintenance = 1910; adding new pole = 1926) |

In practice, many OSP projects involve both types of work (installing new conduit in an existing conduit bank is construction; testing the existing cable while working is maintenance). When both apply, the more protective standard governs the hazardous activity. [29 CFR 1910.5(c); BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]

**Critical scope reminder:** This lesson locates the code rules. Zero field safety execution — Topic 9 owns procedures. Citing 1910.146 in a design submittal or on an inspection drawing is appropriate; administering the permit-required confined space program is T9 content.

### 29 CFR 1910 — General Industry

**Subpart S — Electrical:**

29 CFR 1910 Subpart S (§§1910.301–1910.399) covers electrical safety for general-industry workers. Relevant to OSP maintenance work near energized conductors:

- **§1910.333** — Selection and use of work practices (approach distances for qualified/unqualified workers near energized equipment)
- **§1910.335** — Safeguards for personnel protection (insulating equipment, safety signs, barriers)

On joint-use pole maintenance (existing pole, not new installation), the Subpart S approach distances govern the telecom worker's proximity to supply conductors on the pole. These distances parallel the NESC Rule 420–424 approach-distance requirements covered in L4.4 — both set floors; the more restrictive governs. [29 CFR 1910 Subpart S; NESC Rules 420–424]

**§1910.146 — Permit-Required Confined Spaces:**

This is the most frequently cited OSHA section for OSP underground work. A permit-required confined space (PRCS) is a space that:

1. Is large enough for a worker to enter and perform work
2. Has limited or restricted means of entry or exit
3. Is not designed for continuous employee occupancy
4. **AND** contains or has potential to contain a serious safety or health hazard (atmospheric hazard, engulfment hazard, or entrapment configuration)

**For OSP work, splice vaults, manholes, and handholes that are enclosed are permit-required confined spaces** when they meet the above definition. This is not a close call — an enclosed splice vault with a lid is a PRCS by definition in most OSP applications. The atmospheric hazard (oxygen deficiency, combustible gases from the soil or existing infrastructure) is the most common trigger.

The §1910.146 program requirements include:

- **Written PRCS program** maintained by the employer
- **Atmospheric testing** before and during entry (oxygen level, combustible gas, toxic contaminants)
- **Entry permit** for each entry — signed by the entry supervisor, specifying authorized entrants, attendant, time limits, and emergency rescue procedures
- **Attendant** stationed at the space entry point during the entire entry — never enters the space
- **Emergency rescue** capability — either on-site rescue team or rescue service on call with response time that does not create unacceptable risk

[29 CFR 1910.146; BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]

### 29 CFR 1926 — Construction

**Subpart K — Electrical (Construction):**

29 CFR 1926 Subpart K (§§1926.400–1926.449) governs electrical safety for construction workers. Relevant to new OSP construction:

- **§1926.416** — General requirements (approach distances, insulation, lockout/tagout)
- **§1926.417** — Lockout and tagging of circuits

On new aerial construction where the installer works near existing energized supply conductors (stringing a new fiber cable along an existing joint-use route), Subpart K governs the construction crew's approach to energized conductors — including any crossovers above energized lines and all work within the minimum approach distance (MAD) of energized supply lines. [29 CFR 1926 Subpart K]

**Subpart V — Power Transmission and Distribution (Construction):**

29 CFR 1926 Subpart V (§§1926.950–1926.968) governs work on power transmission and distribution lines and equipment. While primarily directed at electrical line workers, Subpart V applies to OSP construction workers whenever they work on, near, or under energized transmission or distribution lines — including new aerial cable installation on joint-use poles shared with transmission or distribution conductors. Key provisions:

- **§1926.960** — Approach distances (minimum air insulation distances from energized conductors — the "bubble" around an energized line within which uninsulated tools and workers may not enter without qualified-worker protection)
- **§1926.964** — Live-line tools and insulated aerial lifts

For OSP construction on joint-use poles: the NESC approach distances (Rules 420–424, covered in L4.4) and the 1926 Subpart V approach distances both apply. The more protective value governs. In most practical cases, 1926 Subpart V distances are derived from NESC, making the two systems consistent. [29 CFR 1926 Subpart V; NESC Rules 420–424]

### OSP Work Scenario — Code Citation Matrix

The following matrix maps common OSP work activities to the governing OSHA section and the topic that covers procedure execution:

| Work activity | OSHA section | Procedure topic |
|---|---|---|
| Entry into splice vault / manhole | §1910.146 (PRCS) | T9 L9.1 |
| Aerial construction near supply conductors (new route) | 1926 Subpart K; 1926 Subpart V §960 | T9 L9.3 |
| Maintenance on joint-use poles (existing installation) | 1910 Subpart S §333 | T9 L9.3 |
| Directional bore under road (new construction) | 1926 Subpart K | T9 |
| Working in manholes on existing cable plant | §1910.146 (PRCS) | T9 L9.1 |

The code citation (part and section) belongs in design specifications, safety plans, and project documentation. The procedures (how to test the atmosphere, how to issue the entry permit, how to maintain the minimum approach distance) belong in Topic 9. [BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8; RUS Bulletin 1751F-630 §2.2]

### AHJ and State OSHA Plan Caveat

OSHA's federal standards represent the minimum. Twenty-two states and two territories operate **State Plans** — OSHA-approved programs with authority to set and enforce their own occupational safety standards. State Plan standards must be at least as effective as federal OSHA standards; they may be more stringent. OSP designers and contractors working in State Plan states must confirm that the applicable state standard matches or exceeds the federal CFR sections cited above. [BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]

## Key Terms (Flashcard Candidates)

**29 CFR 1910 (general industry)**
OSHA standards governing occupational safety for maintenance, operation, and inspection of existing facilities. Applicability trigger for OSP work: maintenance or inspection of an existing cable plant. Key sections: Subpart S §1910.333 (electrical approach distances); §1910.146 (permit-required confined spaces). [29 CFR 1910]

**29 CFR 1926 (construction)**
OSHA standards governing safety for new construction activities. Applicability trigger for OSP work: installation of new cable plant, conduit, aerial strand, or new pole attachments. Key sections: Subpart K §1926.416 (electrical approach); Subpart V §1926.960 (approach distances near energized transmission/distribution lines). [29 CFR 1926]

**29 CFR 1910.146 — Permit-Required Confined Space**
OSHA standard governing entry into spaces that are large enough to enter, have limited entry/exit, are not designed for occupancy, and contain a serious hazard. Requires a written PRCS program, atmospheric testing, entry permit, a dedicated attendant, and emergency rescue capability. Applies to OSP splice vaults and enclosed manholes. Procedures: T9 L9.1. [29 CFR 1910.146]

**29 CFR 1926 Subpart V**
OSHA construction standard for work near energized power transmission and distribution lines. §1926.960 governs minimum approach distances (MAD) — the air-insulation clearance bubble within which unqualified workers cannot approach without live-line tools. Applies to aerial OSP construction on joint-use poles. [29 CFR 1926 Subpart V]

**State Plan (OSHA)**
State-administered occupational safety program approved by federal OSHA as at least as effective as federal standards. Operates in 22 states and 2 territories. May specify standards more stringent than federal CFR. Always confirm applicable State Plan requirements for the project jurisdiction. [BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]

## Multiple-Choice Quiz

---

**Q1.** An OSP crew is installing a new 2-in. conduit and pulling new fiber cable along a rural route — new construction, not maintenance. Which OSHA part governs this work?

- A) 29 CFR 1910 — general industry
- B) 29 CFR 1926 — construction **[CORRECT]**
- C) Both 29 CFR 1910 and 1926 equally — the contractor chooses which to apply
- D) Neither — OSHA standards do not apply to telecommunications outside plant work

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** 29 CFR 1910 governs general industry work — maintenance and operation of existing facilities. New conduit installation and new cable placement constitute **construction** — not general industry maintenance. The applicability trigger is the nature of the work (new installation vs. maintenance), not the industry sector. [29 CFR 1910; 29 CFR 1926; BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]
- **B — Correct.** New conduit installation and cable placement are **construction activities** governed by **29 CFR 1926**. The OSHA trigger is the nature of the work: new installation = construction = Part 1926. Within Part 1926, Subpart K (electrical safety for construction) and, where applicable, Subpart V (power transmission/distribution near energized conductors) apply. [29 CFR 1926 Subpart K; BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]
- **C — Incorrect.** The contractor does not choose which standard applies — the standard is determined by the nature of the work. Where both 1910 and 1926 apply to different activities on the same worksite, each applies to its respective activity type, and the more protective standard governs the hazardous activity when both could apply. [29 CFR 1910.5(c); 29 CFR 1926]
- **D — Incorrect.** OSHA standards fully apply to telecommunications outside plant work. OSP construction crews are covered under 29 CFR 1926 for new construction activities; maintenance crews are covered under 29 CFR 1910. [29 CFR 1910; 29 CFR 1926]

**Q2.** [Applied scenario] A field crew is scheduled to enter an enclosed splice vault to re-splice an existing fiber cable on an in-service cable plant. The crew leader identifies two applicable safety requirements: (1) the vault has a permanent lid and limited entry, and (2) the vault is adjacent to an underground electrical duct bank. Which OSHA section applies to the vault entry, and what additional resource handles the entry procedure?

- A) 29 CFR 1926 Subpart V applies; entry procedure is in L4.4
- B) 29 CFR 1910.146 (permit-required confined space) applies; entry procedure is in T9 L9.1 **[CORRECT]**
- C) 29 CFR 1910.333 applies because the crew is working near an electrical duct bank; entry procedure is in L4.7
- D) No OSHA section applies to fiber splicing in a vault — OSHA governs only electrical work in confined spaces

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** Re-splicing on an existing cable plant is **maintenance / general industry work** governed by 29 CFR **1910**, not 1926 (construction). 1926 Subpart V governs work near energized power transmission and distribution lines during construction — not confined space entry for fiber maintenance. [29 CFR 1910.146; 29 CFR 1926 Subpart V]
- **B — Correct.** Two analysis points: (1) Work type: re-splicing an existing cable plant is **general industry maintenance** → 29 CFR 1910 governs. (2) Hazard: an enclosed splice vault with limited entry adjacent to an electrical duct bank is a **permit-required confined space** per 29 CFR 1910.146 — it is large enough to enter, has limited entry/exit, is not designed for occupancy, and has potential atmospheric and electrical hazards. §1910.146 requires atmospheric testing, an entry permit, a dedicated attendant outside the space, and emergency rescue capability. Entry procedure execution is **T9 L9.1** — this lesson provides the code citation only. [29 CFR 1910.146; T9 L9.1; BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]
- **C — Incorrect.** §1910.333 (approach distances for general industry workers near energized equipment) applies to the proximity of the crew to energized electrical equipment, not to the vault entry hazard itself. The primary hazard from the vault entry is confined space (limited entry/exit + potential atmospheric hazard) — §1910.146 governs. §1910.333 may also apply if the crew works near energized equipment inside the vault, but it is not the primary citation for the vault entry. L4.7 covers NEC Article 250 grounding — not OSHA confined space procedures. [29 CFR 1910.146; 29 CFR 1910.333]
- **D — Incorrect.** OSHA §1910.146 applies to all permit-required confined spaces, not only those with electrical hazards. Any confined space with a serious safety or health hazard — atmospheric (oxygen deficiency, toxic gas), engulfment, or entrapment — is covered. Fiber splicing work in a vault creates atmospheric exposure risk (e.g., methane from adjacent soil, oxygen depletion in sealed vaults) regardless of whether energized electrical equipment is present. [29 CFR 1910.146]

**Q3.** A joint-use pole maintenance crew is relocating an existing fiber cable attachment on an existing pole — no new poles, no new construction, maintenance scope only. The pole is shared with a 7.2 kV distribution supply conductor. Which OSHA section provides the approach-distance requirements for the telecom worker on this pole?

- A) 29 CFR 1926 Subpart V §1926.960 — construction approach distances
- B) 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S §1910.333 — general industry electrical approach distances **[CORRECT]**
- C) 29 CFR 1910.146 — confined space rules govern work at height on poles
- D) No OSHA approach-distance requirement applies; NESC Rules 420–424 are the exclusive standard

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** 29 CFR 1926 Subpart V governs construction work near energized transmission and distribution lines — new installation, not maintenance of existing attachments. Maintenance relocation of an existing attachment on an existing pole is general industry work, governed by 29 CFR 1910. [29 CFR 1926 Subpart V; 29 CFR 1910]
- **B — Correct.** Maintenance relocation of an existing cable attachment on an existing joint-use pole is **general industry maintenance** → 29 CFR **1910** governs. 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S §**1910.333** covers selection and use of work practices by workers near energized electrical equipment, including approach distances for workers who are not qualified for the supply-conductor voltage level. The 7.2 kV distribution conductor requires compliance with §1910.333 approach distances in addition to NESC Rules 420–424 (L4.4). Both sets of approach distances apply; the more protective governs. [29 CFR 1910 Subpart S §1910.333; BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]
- **C — Incorrect.** §1910.146 governs permit-required confined spaces — enclosed spaces with limited entry and serious hazards. An aerial pole is not a confined space. Pole-top work has its own hazards (fall, electrical approach) governed by §1910.333 and the fall protection provisions of 1910 Subpart D. [29 CFR 1910.146; 29 CFR 1910.333]
- **D — Incorrect.** NESC Rules 420–424 and OSHA §1910.333 both apply and are not mutually exclusive. NESC sets engineering minimums; OSHA sets worker safety minimums. For a worker on a joint-use pole with a 7.2 kV supply conductor, both must be satisfied simultaneously. Where the two conflict, the more protective governs. [29 CFR 1910 Subpart S; NESC Rules 420–424; L4.4]

**Q4.** Which OSHA subpart and CFR section governs approach distances for an OSP construction crew stringing new aerial fiber cable on new joint-use poles that share the route with an existing 69 kV transmission line?

- A) 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S — §1910.333 (general industry electrical)
- B) 29 CFR 1926 Subpart V — §1926.960 (construction approach distances near power transmission lines) **[CORRECT]**
- C) 29 CFR 1910.146 — permit-required confined space (transmission-line voltage creates a confined-hazard zone)
- D) No OSHA requirement — 69 kV transmission lines are regulated exclusively by FERC, not OSHA

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** New aerial fiber cable installation on new joint-use poles is **construction** → 29 CFR 1926 governs. 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S governs general industry (maintenance). The distinction between new installation (1926) and maintenance (1910) is the core applicability trigger. [29 CFR 1926; 29 CFR 1910]
- **B — Correct.** New aerial fiber installation on new poles is a construction activity → 29 CFR **1926** governs. The 69 kV transmission line creates an energized power transmission hazard → 29 CFR 1926 **Subpart V** (§§1926.950–1926.968) applies. **§1926.960** specifically governs approach distances — the minimum air-insulation distance (minimum approach distance, or MAD) within which construction workers may not approach energized conductors without insulated live-line tools and qualified-worker authorization. At 69 kV, the MAD is significant (several feet for qualified workers; much greater for unqualified workers). [29 CFR 1926 Subpart V §1926.960; BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]
- **C — Incorrect.** §1910.146 governs permit-required confined spaces — a physical space configuration (limited entry, enclosed, serious hazard). A transmission line does not create a confined space; aerial work near transmission lines is governed by approach-distance standards, not confined space standards. [29 CFR 1910.146; 29 CFR 1926 Subpart V]
- **D — Incorrect.** OSHA has regulatory authority over worker safety on construction sites including those adjacent to FERC-jurisdictional transmission facilities. FERC governs the grid reliability and permitting of the transmission system; OSHA governs the safety of construction workers working near it. The two are not in conflict; both apply. [29 CFR 1926 Subpart V]

**Q5.** A safety manager asks whether OSHA Part 1910 or Part 1926 governs OSP work "when both construction and maintenance are happening on the same project at the same site." What is the correct answer?

- A) Part 1910 supersedes Part 1926 whenever maintenance of an existing cable plant is in progress — all work on the site shifts to 1910
- B) Part 1926 supersedes Part 1910 whenever new construction is underway — all work on the site shifts to 1926
- C) Each standard applies to its respective activity type; where both could govern the same hazardous activity, the more protective standard applies **[CORRECT]**
- D) The contractor selects which standard applies to the entire site — they cannot operate under both simultaneously

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** Maintenance work does not cause all construction activities on the site to shift to 1910. Part 1910 and 1926 govern based on activity type, not site-wide — the maintenance crew follows 1910 for their activities; the construction crew follows 1926 for theirs. [29 CFR 1910; 29 CFR 1926]
- **B — Incorrect.** The presence of construction does not shift all work on the site to Part 1926. The activity type at each work point determines the applicable standard. Maintenance crews on the same site as a construction crew do not become "construction workers" by proximity. [29 CFR 1910; 29 CFR 1926]
- **C — Correct.** 29 CFR 1910.5(c) addresses overlap: when an activity is covered by both 1910 and 1926, compliance with 1926 is deemed compliance with 1910 for that activity — but the reverse is not always true. The correct operating principle: each standard applies to its respective activity; when both could apply to the same hazardous activity (e.g., a crew working on both new conduit installation and existing cable maintenance in the same vault), the more protective of the two standards governs that specific hazardous activity. [29 CFR 1910.5(c); BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.8]
- **D — Incorrect.** Contractors operating at a site with mixed construction and maintenance activities routinely operate under both 1910 and 1926 simultaneously — different crews, different activities, different governing standards. OSHA explicitly anticipates this in 29 CFR 1910.5(c). [29 CFR 1910.5(c)]

## Final Check: Pulse Questions Before Lesson 4.14

**Pulse 1.** Identify the OSHA CFR part, subpart, and section that governs the following scenario: An OSP crew performs routine monthly inspection of an existing buried splice vault, entering with test equipment to OTDR-test the existing cable. The vault has a steel bolted lid and is adjacent to a live electrical duct bank.

*Expected answer:* Work type: inspection of existing cable plant = **general industry maintenance** → 29 CFR **1910** governs. Hazard: steel bolted lid + limited entry + adjacent electrical duct bank + potential atmospheric hazard (oxygen deficiency, methane) → **permit-required confined space** → **29 CFR 1910.146**. Requirements invoked: atmospheric testing before entry, written entry permit, attendant stationed outside the space, emergency rescue capability. Field procedure execution: T9 L9.1. [29 CFR 1910.146; T9 L9.1]

**Pulse 2.** A construction crew on a new aerial fiber route is working within 30 feet of a 115 kV transmission line. Which OSHA part and subpart govern the approach distance requirements? How does the applicability differ if the same crew is also performing maintenance on an existing fiber cable on the same route the same day?

*Expected answer:* For **new aerial installation** near the 115 kV line: construction activity → **29 CFR 1926 Subpart V §1926.960** governs approach distances (minimum approach distance / MAD for 115 kV). For **maintenance work on existing cable** (same crew, same day): maintenance → **29 CFR 1910 Subpart S §1910.333** governs approach distances for the maintenance activity. Each standard applies to its respective activity type; both may be in effect simultaneously on the same worksite. The more protective standard governs where both could apply to the same hazardous activity. [29 CFR 1926 Subpart V §1926.960; 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S §1910.333]
