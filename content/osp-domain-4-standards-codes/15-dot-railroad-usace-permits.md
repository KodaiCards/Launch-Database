# DOT, Railroad, and USACE Permits

## Learning Objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the learner will be able to:

- Identify the federal regulatory authority governing state highway, railroad, and water crossings and cite the applicable CFR part for each crossing type
- Apply NWP 12 criteria to determine whether a water crossing qualifies for the nationwide permit or requires an Individual Permit, including the 0.1-acre permanent fill limit and the regional suspension caveat
- Explain NHPA Section 106 (54 U.S.C. § 306108) consultation requirements — specifically SHPO and THPO coordination — and articulate why RUS-funded projects treat Section 106 clearance as a hard prerequisite to construction start
- Construct a 3-crossing permit timeline matrix for a route crossing a state highway, a short-line railroad, and a navigable creek — identifying agency, permit type, and approximate lead time at kickoff

---

## Reading Content

### Why Crossing Permits Are Always on the Critical Path

A fiber route that crosses a state highway, railroad, or navigable waterway cannot begin construction on that crossing without a permit from the controlling agency. These permits are not obtained overnight — they involve engineering review, agency approvals, sometimes environmental assessment, and in the case of Class I railroads, real estate department coordination before the permit process even begins. If permits are not initiated at project kickoff, they will delay construction. In the experience of OSP firms working on rural RUS routes, crossing permits — not material procurement, not labor availability — are the most common cause of schedule delays. Identify every crossing in desk research (T3 L3.1), map them to their permit type at route planning, and initiate the permit applications at the earliest possible moment. [RUS Bulletin 1751F-630 §7; BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.10; T3 L3.1 cross-reference]

The lead-time table in this lesson (and the companion table in T3 L3.8) are planning tools — not guarantees. Actual lead times vary by agency, reviewer workload, and project complexity. The values below are the field-validated ranges used in OSP project scheduling.

### State Highway Crossings — 23 CFR Part 645

Federal-aid highways (Interstates, US routes, state routes on the federal-aid system) are governed by **23 CFR Part 645** — "Utilities Accommodation on Federal-Aid Highway Projects." Part 645 establishes the policy framework under which state DOTs must manage utility permits along federal-aid highways. Individual states implement Part 645 through their own utility accommodation policies (often called a Utility Accommodation Policy or UAP), which may be more stringent than the federal minimum.

**Key permit elements for OSP crossings:**

- **Bore only** on Interstate and NHS routes — open-cut is generally prohibited
- **State arterial crossings:** most state DOTs prefer bore; open-cut may be permitted with full lane overlay and traffic control plan
- **County and local roads:** permit issued by the county road authority; open-cut allowed with standard pavement restoration in most jurisdictions

**Permit timeline:**
- State DOT permit (primary arterials): 30–90 days typical
- County road permit: 5–20 days typical
- Interstate / NHS: may require FHWA concurrence in addition to state DOT approval — add 30–60 days if FHWA concurrence is needed

**23 CFR Part 645 Subpart B** (23 CFR 645.211 et seq.) governs the specific conditions for utility accommodation on federally funded highways. OSP conduit below Interstate ROW must meet Part 645 requirements for construction method, burial depth, and restoration. [23 CFR Part 645; AASHTO Utility Accommodation Policy Manual; T3 L3.8 cross-reference]

### Railroad Crossings — AAR Standards and Carrier Permits

**No CFR part governs railroad crossing permits directly** — railroads are private property owners and private carriers. The applicable standards are set by each railroad company, with guidance from the Association of American Railroads (AAR) Engineering Standards. The FRA (Federal Railroad Administration) regulates railroad safety, including excavation near tracks; but the crossing permit itself is issued by the railroad company.

**Universal requirements (all railroads, all classes):**

1. **Written permission from the railroad company** — every crossing requires a signed agreement with the railroad's real estate or ROW department before construction begins
2. **Bore only, no open-cut** — destabilizing the track sub-ballast creates a derailment risk; open-cut under any active railroad track is a non-negotiable prohibition
3. **Casing pipe** — most carrier permits require a steel casing pipe at the railroad-specified wall thickness; HDPE without casing is typically not accepted on railroad crossings
4. **Railroad inspector / flagman** — the railroad requires a company flagman present during all work within the railroad ROW; flagman costs are billed to the applicant at railroad rates
5. **Bore must approach at or near perpendicular** to the track centerline

**Class structure and lead times (cross-reference T3 L3.8 lead-time table):**

| Railroad class | Examples | Permit lead time |
|---|---|---|
| Class III (short-line / regional) | Short-line carriers serving rural corridors | **30–60 days** |
| Class I (major freight carriers) | BNSF, CSX, Norfolk Southern, Union Pacific | **90–180 days** |

**Short-line railroad permit — Primary worked example:**

Short-line (Class III) railroads are the primary crossing type on rural RUS routes in regions like central Georgia — PSC's service territory. A typical short-line crossing process:

1. **Contact the railroad's engineering or ROW department** — identify the correct contact (short-line carriers often have small engineering staffs; phone contact may be faster than online submissions)
2. **Submit crossing application** — include: crossing location (milepost + county), bore plan (HDD or pneumatic), casing pipe spec, depth below rail, applicant contact info, construction insurance certificate
3. **Railroad reviews and issues permit** (30–60 days) — permit specifies: burial depth below top of rail, casing pipe wall thickness, bore angle (perpendicular preferred), ROW boundary-to-boundary casing requirement, flagman requirement, restoration
4. **Flagman coordination** — schedule flagman through the railroad's operations department before construction starts; flagman must be present throughout all work within the railroad's ROW
5. **Bore and restore** — per permit conditions
6. **Close-out** — submit as-built bore log and restoration report to railroad; obtain written confirmation of close-out; include in RUS Form 219 close-out package if RUS-funded

**Typical short-line crossing specification:** Casing pipe from ROW boundary to ROW boundary (often 50–80 ft total), steel casing at ≥ ¼-in. wall thickness, minimum 48-in. depth below top of rail (confirm with individual carrier — some require 60 in.), bore angle ≤ 30° from perpendicular. [AAR Engineering Standards; RUS Bulletin 1751F-630 §7; T3 L3.8]

**Class I railroad — Appendix scenario (contrast):**

Class I railroad crossings involve multiple layers of bureaucracy that do not exist at short-line carriers. The Class I process may include:

- Real estate right-of-entry agreement (before the technical permit process begins) — adds 30–60 days
- Engineering review by the railroad's signal department (FRA Section 214 coordination if within 25 ft of signal equipment)
- Formal application through the railroad's utility crossing portal (BNSF, CSX, NS, UP each have different online systems)
- Total timeline: **90–180 days** before construction can begin on the crossing

For scheduling purposes: initiate Class I railroad permit applications at project kickoff, before design is complete. Waiting for final drawings before applying will guarantee a schedule delay. [BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.10; T3 L3.8]

### Water Crossings — USACE and NWP 12

Water crossings of navigable waterways require authorization from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) under **33 CFR Parts 320–332** (General Regulatory Policies for Permits for Work in Navigable Waters). Two authorization tracks exist:

**Track 1 — Nationwide Permit (NWP):** Pre-authorized activities that meet specific criteria; require notification but not individual case-by-case review. Faster.

**Track 2 — Individual Permit (IP):** Full case-by-case review; required when NWP criteria are not met or when NWP conditions are exceeded. Much slower — typically 6–12 months.

**Nationwide Permit 12 (NWP 12) — Utility Line Activities:**

NWP 12 authorizes utility line installation (including fiber optic cables in conduit) in waters of the United States, subject to conditions. The two most critical conditions for OSP projects:

**Condition 1 — Permanent fill limit: 0.1 acre**

NWP 12 authorizes utility line crossings with permanent loss of waters of the United States not exceeding **0.1 acre** (approximately 4,356 sq ft) of permanent fill. For typical OSP bore crossings under streams or wetlands, the fill is the bore pit and trench at the water's edge — the bore itself (below the stream bed) does not count as "fill." A small stream crossing with a compact bore pit may stay well under 0.1 acre. A crossing involving significant wetland fill or a wide floodplain trench may exceed the limit.

If a crossing generates > 0.1 acre of permanent fill → **NWP 12 does not apply** → Individual Permit required.

**Condition 2 — Regional suspension caveat:**

NWP 12 is subject to regional suspension or modification by individual USACE districts. A USACE district (e.g., Savannah District for much of Georgia, Mobile District for Alabama) may suspend NWP 12 for specific activity types in specific geographic areas — typically where regional environmental sensitivity (endangered species, wetland type, stream class) requires case-by-case review that NWP cannot provide. Always confirm NWP 12 is active in the applicable USACE district before designing a project schedule that relies on NWP 12 authorization. If NWP 12 is suspended in the project district for the crossing type: Individual Permit required. [33 CFR Part 330; USACE NWP 12 conditions; T3 L3.8 cross-reference]

**Pre-Construction Notification (PCN):** Even when NWP 12 applies, most utility line crossings of navigable waters require a **Pre-Construction Notification** to the applicable USACE district. The PCN must be submitted and the USACE must respond (or the 45-day review period must expire) before construction begins. Include PCN preparation and the 45-day window in the project schedule. [33 CFR Part 330]

**Section 401 Water Quality Certification (401 WQC):** NWP 12 requires Section 401 WQC from the applicable state environmental agency (e.g., Georgia EPD). The state may have conditions or may deny certification, which would invalidate NWP 12 authorization for that crossing. Initiate 401 WQC coordination simultaneously with the USACE PCN. [33 CFR Part 330]

**Navigability confirmation:** Before applying the simpler NWP framework, confirm whether the waterway is federally navigable (Lesson 3.3 / T3 cross-reference). A waterway that looks non-navigable in the field may be federally designated as navigable based on historical use. Contact the applicable USACE district to confirm navigability status for any project crossing a waterway.

### NHPA Section 106 — Historic Preservation Consultation

**Statutory citation:** **Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act**, codified at **54 U.S.C. § 306108**.

**When it triggers:** Section 106 review is triggered whenever a **federal action** (or a non-federal action with federal nexus — such as a USACE permit, a federal-aid highway permit, or a RUS loan) has the potential to affect properties listed on or eligible for the National Register of Historic Places (NRHP). The "federal nexus" trigger is the key: a private fiber project with no federal involvement does not trigger Section 106. A RUS-funded fiber project with a USACE permit does.

**Why every RUS-funded rural OSP project triggers Section 106:**

RUS loan financing = federal action. Any RUS-funded project with ROW that may affect NRHP-listed or eligible properties (prehistoric sites, historic structures, traditional cultural properties, battlefields) triggers Section 106. This is not uncommon on rural routes — many rural areas contain unidentified archaeological sites, and rural cemeteries, farmsteads, and historic districts are frequently NRHP-eligible.

**Who is consulted:**

- **SHPO (State Historic Preservation Office):** The state agency responsible for NRHP nominations and Section 106 consultation. For a RUS-funded project in Georgia: the Georgia Historic Preservation Division (HPD). The lead federal agency (RUS or the USACE, depending on which federal nexus triggers Section 106) consults with the SHPO.
- **THPO (Tribal Historic Preservation Office):** The equivalent historic preservation authority for federally recognized tribes with ancestral ties to the project area. If the project ROW crosses lands within a tribe's area of interest (which may include much of rural Georgia for some southeastern tribes), the THPO must be notified and consulted in parallel with SHPO. **THPO consultation is not optional when tribal interests are present** — failure to consult with an eligible THPO is a Section 106 violation that can halt construction and require mitigation.

**Section 106 process:**

1. **Initiation:** Lead agency (RUS or USACE) notifies SHPO and identifies tribes for THPO notification
2. **Area of Potential Effects (APE) determination:** Define the geographic area where the project may affect historic properties (the ROW plus a buffer for indirect effects)
3. **Identification and evaluation:** Archaeological survey (Phase I, and Phase II if warranted) to identify resources within the APE; evaluate NRHP eligibility
4. **Assessment of adverse effect:** Does the project affect identified historic properties? If yes → proceed to resolution. If no adverse effect → proceed with conditions
5. **Resolution:** If adverse effect is found, negotiate a Programmatic Agreement (PA) or Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) with SHPO/THPO to mitigate the effect (avoidance, data recovery, protective measures)

**Hard prerequisite for RUS-funded projects:** For RUS-funded projects, Section 106 clearance (no adverse effect finding, or MOA executed) is a **hard prerequisite to construction start** — construction cannot begin while Section 106 is open. This is not a soft timeline constraint; it is a loan condition. A contractor who begins construction before Section 106 is resolved on a RUS project risks loan suspension and required mitigation at their expense. [NHPA §106 (54 U.S.C. § 306108); RUS Bulletin 1751F-630 §7; T3 L3.1 cross-reference; T3 L3.11 cross-reference]

### 3-Crossing Scenario: Permit Matrix + Timeline

**Route:** A rural RUS-funded fiber route (PSC telecom program) crosses: (1) a state arterial road, (2) a short-line railroad, and (3) a navigable creek with 0.05 acres of permanent fill at the bore pit. The route ROW has a potential historic farmstead within 150 feet. Project kickoff: Day 0.

| Crossing/Issue | Agency | Permit/Authorization | Lead time | Initiate at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| State arterial road | State DOT | Utility accommodation permit (23 CFR 645) | 30–90 days | Day 0 |
| Short-line railroad | Railroad company | Crossing agreement + flagman coordination (AAR standards) | **30–60 days** | Day 0 |
| Navigable creek (0.05 acres fill) | USACE + State EPD | NWP 12 (qualifies: ≤ 0.1 acre fill) + PCN + 401 WQC | 45-day PCN review + 401 coordination | Day 0 |
| NHPA Section 106 (RUS nexus) | RUS + SHPO + THPO | Section 106 consultation — APE survey → no adverse effect or MOA | 60–120 days (varies with survey findings) | Day 0 — hard prerequisite; no construction until cleared |

**Scheduling observation:** At Day 0, **all four actions initiate simultaneously**. The railroad permit (30–60 days) and Section 106 consultation (60–120 days) typically control the schedule — the DOT permit and USACE PCN may resolve sooner. Construction on the railroad crossing cannot start until the railroad agreement is signed. **Construction nowhere on the route** can start until Section 106 is cleared (RUS loan condition).

**Class I railroad appendix contrast:** If the crossing were a Class I railroad instead of short-line: permit lead time **90–180 days**, initiation of real estate right-of-entry process at Day 0 (before engineering is even complete), formal online application to carrier's utility crossing portal. Section 106 remains the same — federal nexus (RUS) applies regardless of railroad class. The Class I railroad permit, not Section 106, would likely control the schedule. [T3 L3.8 cross-reference for lead-time table; NHPA §106 (54 U.S.C. § 306108)]

## Key Terms (Flashcard Candidates)

**23 CFR Part 645**
Federal regulation governing utility accommodation on federal-aid highway projects. Establishes the policy framework that state DOTs implement through their utility accommodation policies. Bore required on Interstate and NHS routes; open-cut may be permitted on county roads. State DOT permit lead time: 30–90 days. [23 CFR Part 645]

**NWP 12 (Nationwide Permit 12)**
USACE pre-authorized permit for utility line activities (including OSP fiber) in waters of the United States. Conditions: permanent fill ≤ 0.1 acre; PCN required for most utility line crossings; 401 WQC from state environmental agency; **regional suspension caveat** — confirm NWP 12 is active in the applicable USACE district before relying on it. Exceeds 0.1 acre fill → Individual Permit required (6–12 months). [33 CFR Part 330; USACE NWP 12]

**Individual Permit (USACE)**
Case-by-case USACE authorization under Section 404 of the Clean Water Act and/or Section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act. Required when NWP 12 is not available (fill > 0.1 acre, regional suspension, or particularly sensitive resources). Typical lead time: 6–12 months. [33 CFR Parts 320–332]

**NHPA Section 106 (54 U.S.C. § 306108)**
Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act. Requires federal agencies (and non-federal applicants with a federal nexus) to consult with SHPO and THPO before initiating any undertaking that may affect properties listed or eligible for the National Register of Historic Places. RUS-funded projects trigger Section 106 through the federal loan nexus. Hard prerequisite to construction start on RUS projects. [NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108]

**SHPO (State Historic Preservation Office)**
The state agency responsible for NRHP nominations and Section 106 consultation. In Georgia: Georgia Historic Preservation Division (HPD). Must be notified and consulted as part of Section 106 review before construction begins on a RUS-funded project that may affect historic properties. [NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108]

**THPO (Tribal Historic Preservation Office)**
The historic preservation authority for federally recognized tribes with ancestral interests in the project area. Must be consulted in parallel with SHPO when tribal interests are present within the Area of Potential Effects. THPO consultation is mandatory, not optional, when the tribe is eligible and has expressed interest in the project area. [NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108]

**Short-line (Class III) railroad crossing**
Railroad permit for regional/short-line carriers. Lead time: 30–60 days (primary worked example for rural RUS routes). Requirements: written agreement with railroad, bore only, steel casing from ROW to ROW, railroad flagman during construction. Primary crossing type on rural fiber routes in regions like central Georgia. [AAR Engineering Standards; T3 L3.8]

**Class I railroad crossing**
Railroad permit for major freight carriers (BNSF, CSX, NS, UP). Lead time: 90–180 days. Additional steps beyond short-line: real estate right-of-entry agreement (before permit process), online permit portal submission, signal department review if within 25 ft of signal equipment. Initiate at project kickoff, before design is complete. [AAR Engineering Standards; T3 L3.8]

**Q1.** A RUS-funded fiber route includes an NWP 12 PCN submittal for a crossing of a navigable creek. The fill area at the bore pit is calculated at 0.12 acres. Does NWP 12 authorize this crossing?

- A) Yes — NWP 12 applies to all OSP utility line crossings in navigable waterways, regardless of fill area
- B) No — the permanent fill area of 0.12 acres exceeds the NWP 12 limit of 0.1 acre; an Individual Permit is required **[CORRECT]**
- C) Yes — 0.12 acres is within the 0.25-acre NWP 12 fill limit
- D) No — NWP 12 does not apply to any RUS-funded projects; a Section 404 Individual Permit is always required

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** NWP 12 does not apply to all OSP crossings regardless of fill — it has a permanent fill limit of 0.1 acre (approximately 4,356 sq ft) of permanent loss of waters of the United States. Crossings that exceed this limit require an Individual Permit, not NWP 12 authorization. [33 CFR Part 330; USACE NWP 12]
- **B — Correct.** NWP 12 permits permanent loss of waters of the United States not exceeding **0.1 acre**. At 0.12 acres, this crossing exceeds the limit by 0.02 acres. **NWP 12 does not apply** — the project must obtain an Individual Permit under Section 404 of the Clean Water Act (and possibly Section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act if navigable). An Individual Permit typically requires 6–12 months of USACE review. The project schedule must be revised to accommodate this extended permit timeline. [33 CFR Part 330; USACE NWP 12 conditions]
- **C — Incorrect.** The NWP 12 permanent fill limit is **0.1 acre**, not 0.25 acres. At 0.12 acres, the crossing exceeds the 0.1-acre threshold by 0.02 acres. [33 CFR Part 330; USACE NWP 12]
- **D — Incorrect.** RUS funding is not a disqualification for NWP 12 — NWP 12 applies based on the nature of the activity (utility line crossing) and the fill area (≤ 0.1 acre), not on funding source. RUS-funded projects that meet NWP 12 conditions qualify for NWP 12. The RUS nexus does trigger NHPA Section 106 (a separate requirement), but does not prevent NWP 12 use. [33 CFR Part 330; USACE NWP 12]

**Q2.** Which statute requires SHPO and THPO consultation for a RUS-funded OSP project, and what is the consequence of not completing this consultation before construction begins?

- A) 23 CFR Part 645 (federal-aid highway accommodation); the project loses its DOT permit
- B) NHPA Section 106, codified at 54 U.S.C. § 306108; failing to complete Section 106 before construction on a RUS project violates the loan conditions — construction must stop and mitigation may be required **[CORRECT]**
- C) 29 CFR 1926 Subpart V (OSHA construction); the OSHA citation halts work near transmission lines
- D) 7 CFR Part 1755 Subpart D; the contractor is liable for Section 106 unless they file Form 219

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** 23 CFR Part 645 governs utility accommodation on federal-aid highway projects — it does not contain the SHPO/THPO consultation requirement. The Section 106 consultation requirement comes from NHPA Section 106 (54 U.S.C. § 306108), triggered by the RUS federal loan nexus. [NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108; 23 CFR Part 645]
- **B — Correct.** **NHPA Section 106 (54 U.S.C. § 306108)** requires federal agencies (and non-federal applicants with a federal nexus) to consult with SHPO and THPO before initiating any undertaking that may affect properties listed or eligible for the National Register of Historic Places. For RUS-funded projects, the federal loan is the nexus; RUS (as the lead federal agency) conducts the Section 106 consultation. Section 106 clearance is a **hard prerequisite to construction start** on RUS projects — it is a loan condition, not an advisory recommendation. Construction that begins before Section 106 is resolved violates the loan conditions and may require: construction stop, costly mitigation (avoidance, archaeological data recovery), loan suspension, and potential personal liability for the engineer of record. [NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108; RUS Bulletin 1751F-630 §7]
- **C — Incorrect.** 29 CFR 1926 Subpart V governs approach distances for construction workers near energized power transmission lines — it has no SHPO/THPO consultation requirement and is not triggered by historic preservation considerations. [29 CFR 1926 Subpart V]
- **D — Incorrect.** 7 CFR Part 1755 Subpart D is the regulatory authority for RUS OSP construction standards — it does not contain the Section 106 consultation requirement. Form 219 is the materials inventory form, not a Section 106 compliance instrument. The statutory basis for SHPO/THPO consultation is 54 U.S.C. § 306108 (NHPA), not 7 CFR Part 1755. [7 CFR Part 1755; NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108]

**Q3.** A rural OSP route crosses a short-line railroad track. What is the approximate permit lead time, and what is the single most important construction requirement that applies regardless of the railroad's specific permit conditions?

- A) 90–180 days; bore required only on Class I tracks — open-cut acceptable on short-line tracks
- B) 30–60 days; bore required under all railroad tracks — open-cut is a non-negotiable prohibition **[CORRECT]**
- C) 5–20 days; bore or open-cut permitted at the contractor's option for short-line crossings
- D) 30–60 days; USACE Individual Permit required before railroad agreement can be executed

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** Short-line (Class III) railroad crossings have a typical lead time of 30–60 days, not 90–180 days. That range applies to Class I railroads (major freight carriers). More critically, bore is required on **all** railroad crossings regardless of railroad class — open-cut under any active railroad track destabilizes the track sub-ballast and creates a derailment risk; no carrier permits open-cut. [AAR Engineering Standards; T3 L3.8]
- **B — Correct.** Short-line (Class III) railroad permit lead time: **30–60 days** (primary worked example for rural RUS routes). The universal requirement: **bore only, no open-cut** — applies to all railroad tracks regardless of carrier class or track usage level. An open-cut trench under an active railroad track is an FRA safety violation and a non-negotiable prohibition in every railroad company's crossing agreement. The bore must approach near-perpendicular to the track centerline; a steel casing pipe is required from ROW boundary to ROW boundary; a railroad flagman must be present during all work within the railroad's ROW. [AAR Engineering Standards; RUS Bulletin 1751F-630 §7; T3 L3.8]
- **C — Incorrect.** A 5–20 day lead time is characteristic of county road permits, not railroad crossings. Short-line railroads require 30–60 days minimum. Open-cut is not permitted under any railroad track regardless of class — bore is mandatory. [AAR Engineering Standards; T3 L3.8]
- **D — Incorrect.** A USACE Individual Permit is not required before a railroad crossing agreement can be executed — they are separate processes for different agencies covering different activities. The railroad crossing agreement is with the railroad company; USACE permits cover work in navigable waters. A bore crossing under a railroad that does not involve navigable waters does not require a USACE permit. [AAR Engineering Standards; 33 CFR Parts 320–332]

**Q4.** An OSP designer learns that NWP 12 has been regionally suspended by the applicable USACE district for utility line crossings of certain wetland types in that region. The route crosses a wetland of the suspended type with 0.04 acres of fill. Which permit track is required?

- A) NWP 12 still applies — the 0.1-acre fill limit is met, which overrides any regional suspension
- B) An Individual Permit is required — the regional suspension by the USACE district removes NWP 12 authorization for this crossing type regardless of fill area **[CORRECT]**
- C) A PCN is sufficient — regional suspensions apply only to fill areas > 0.5 acres
- D) No USACE permit is required — wetland crossings by OSP conduit are exempt from Section 404 under a blanket utility exemption

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** The fill area limit (0.1 acre) is one condition of NWP 12; the regional suspension is an independent condition. **Both conditions must be met** for NWP 12 to apply. A regional suspension removes NWP 12 authorization for the specified activity type in the district regardless of whether the fill area would otherwise qualify. Once NWP 12 is suspended, the crossing requires an Individual Permit — the fill area limit is irrelevant when the permit itself is unavailable. [33 CFR Part 330; USACE NWP 12]
- **B — Correct.** NWP 12 is subject to **regional suspension or modification** by individual USACE districts for specific resource types or geographic areas where the standard nationwide conditions are insufficient to protect environmental resources. A regional suspension removes NWP 12 as an available authorization pathway for the affected crossing type, regardless of fill area. With NWP 12 unavailable, the project must obtain an **Individual Permit** under Section 404 (and possibly Section 10 for navigable waters) — a process typically requiring 6–12 months. This is the regional suspension caveat that must be verified before finalizing project schedules. [33 CFR Part 330; USACE NWP 12 regional conditions; BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.10]
- **C — Incorrect.** PCN (Pre-Construction Notification) is a notification to the USACE under NWP 12 — it is a step within the NWP 12 process, not an alternative permit track. Where NWP 12 is suspended, PCN cannot substitute for Individual Permit authorization. The regional suspension applies regardless of fill area magnitude. [33 CFR Part 330]
- **D — Incorrect.** There is no blanket exemption from Section 404 for OSP conduit crossings of wetlands. Utility line activities crossing waters of the United States (including wetlands) require USACE authorization. NWP 12 is the typical authorization pathway; when NWP 12 is unavailable (due to regional suspension or exceeded fill area), an Individual Permit is required. [33 CFR Parts 320–332; USACE NWP 12]

**Q5.** A project manager states: "Section 106 consultation with the SHPO can run concurrently with construction — there's no requirement to wait for clearance before breaking ground." Is this correct for a RUS-funded project?

- A) Yes — Section 106 is a notification requirement, not a prerequisite; construction can proceed while consultation is ongoing
- B) No — for RUS-funded projects, Section 106 clearance is a hard prerequisite to construction start; breaking ground before clearance violates the RUS loan conditions **[CORRECT]**
- C) Yes — Section 106 only applies to direct impacts on NRHP-listed properties; if no listed property is identified, construction may proceed
- D) No — but this applies only to the specific crossing with the potential historic farmstead, not to the rest of the route

*Rationale:*
- **A — Incorrect.** Section 106 is not a notification-only requirement — it is a consultation process with a defined sequence (identification, evaluation, assessment of adverse effect, resolution) that must be completed before the federal agency (RUS) can approve the undertaking to proceed. Running construction concurrently with an open Section 106 process is a violation of the NHPA. [NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108]
- **B — Correct.** For **RUS-funded projects**, Section 106 clearance (no adverse effect finding or executed MOA) is a **hard prerequisite to construction start** — it is a loan condition, not a concurrent activity. Breaking ground before Section 106 is resolved violates: (1) NHPA §106 (54 U.S.C. § 306108) — which requires the federal agency to consider effects on historic properties before approving the undertaking; (2) RUS loan conditions — which incorporate Section 106 compliance as a prerequisite to construction authorization. Consequences may include: mandatory construction stop, required archaeological investigation (Phase II/III) at applicant expense, loan suspension, and possible legal liability for the consulting engineer. [NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108; RUS Bulletin 1751F-630 §7; T3 L3.11 cross-reference]
- **C — Incorrect.** Section 106 applies to properties listed on or **eligible** for the NRHP — not only to already-listed properties. Many rural areas contain unidentified archaeological sites that may be NRHP-eligible. The eligibility determination is part of the Section 106 process; assuming no eligible properties exist without conducting the survey and consultation is not a valid exemption. [NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108]
- **D — Incorrect.** Section 106 for a RUS-funded project applies to the entire undertaking — the complete RUS-funded project — not just the portions with identified or suspected historic resources. The Area of Potential Effects (APE) may cover the entire project ROW. Construction on any portion of the project cannot begin until Section 106 is cleared for the complete undertaking. [NHPA §106; 54 U.S.C. § 306108]

## Final Check: Pulse Questions — Topic 4 Close

**Pulse 1.** A 3-crossing rural RUS fiber route has: (1) a state DOT permit, (2) a short-line railroad crossing agreement, and (3) a NWP 12 + PCN + 401 WQC for a navigable creek (0.05 acres fill). Section 106 consultation is also required. At Day 0 (project kickoff), rank the four actions by which is most likely to control the construction start date, assuming normal processing times.

*Expected answer:* Initiating all four at Day 0, rank by expected completion: (1) State DOT permit: 30–90 days (earliest possible completion). (2) Short-line RR agreement: 30–60 days (similar to DOT, flagman scheduling adds time). (3) USACE NWP 12 PCN + 401 WQC: 45-day PCN review + concurrent 401 state coordination — approximately 60–90 days total. (4) **NHPA Section 106: 60–120 days or more depending on survey findings** — typically the controlling constraint. Section 106 is also a hard prerequisite (no construction on any part of the project until cleared), making it the schedule-controlling item on a typical rural RUS route. If the archaeological survey identifies resources requiring Phase II investigation, Section 106 can extend to 6+ months. [NHPA §106; AAR Engineering Standards; 23 CFR Part 645; 33 CFR Part 330; T3 L3.8]

**Pulse 2.** An NWP 12 application is being prepared for a creek crossing. A colleague asks whether the USACE district's regional suspension caveat needs to be checked before the PCN is submitted. Why is this check necessary, and what happens if it is skipped?

*Expected answer:* The regional suspension check must be performed before the PCN because NWP 12 may be suspended by the applicable USACE district for specific activity types or wetland/waterway types in that region. If NWP 12 is suspended in the district for the crossing type, submitting a PCN is meaningless — the USACE district will reject NWP 12 authorization and require an Individual Permit. If this check is skipped: (1) The project schedule assumes a 45-day PCN review window, but an Individual Permit requires 6–12 months — the schedule slips by 5–11 months; (2) Construction cannot begin until USACE authorization is obtained, regardless of other permit completions. The regional suspension caveat is confirmed by contacting the applicable USACE district office or reviewing the district's published NWP condition list before project scheduling is finalized. [33 CFR Part 330; USACE NWP 12; BICSI OSP-DRD Manual, Ch. 2.10]
